# Voice and concision

The voice stays constant across audiences. The tone adapts to the reader's
situation: serious for a failure or data loss, matter-of-fact for reference,
encouraging for a first tutorial.

## Three voice qualities

- **Warm and relaxed.** Natural and conversational, grounded in how people
  actually talk. Contractions, everyday words, and a friendly but not chummy
  register.
- **Crisp and clear.** To the point. Written for scanning first and reading
  second. Simplicity wins over completeness of phrasing.
- **Ready to help.** On the reader's side. Anticipate the real need, and give
  the right information at the moment the reader needs it.

For technical readers, keep the same voice. Assume programming fundamentals,
skip basic concepts, and spend the words on what's specific to the technology.

## Get to the point fast

- Lead with the most important thing: the outcome, the decision, or the action.
- Put the key point where the reader looks first: the first sentence of a
  paragraph, or the first words of a sentence.
- Make choices and next steps obvious.
- Give just enough information to decide or act with confidence, then stop.

## Bigger ideas, fewer words

- Prune every word that adds no meaning. If a sentence reads the same without a
  word, delete the word.
- Prefer one short sentence per idea. Break a long sentence into two.
- Prefer a list or table over a paragraph that enumerates parallel items.
- Cut introductions that restate the heading or title.

| Wordy | Concise |
|---|---|
| If you're ready to purchase the service for your organization, contact your account representative. | Ready to buy? Contact your account representative. |
| Templates provide a starting point for creating new documents. A template can include the styles and layouts that you use frequently. | Save time with a template that includes the styles and layouts you use most often. |

## Write like you speak

- Read the text aloud. Rewrite any sentence you wouldn't say to a colleague.
- Use common contractions: *it's*, *you're*, *don't*, *that's*, *you'll*.
- Prefer short everyday words over formal ones.
- Prefer optimistic, constructive phrasing over warnings, unless the reader
  faces real risk.

## Revise weak writing

- Start most statements and every instruction with a verb.
- Remove *you can* when the sentence works without it.
  - Weak: *You can use the API to list all devices.*
  - Strong: *Use the API to list all devices.* (instruction) or *The API lists
    all devices.* (description)
- Replace *there is*, *there are*, and *there were* with a concrete subject
  and verb.
  - Weak: *There are three settings that control retries.*
  - Strong: *Three settings control retries.*
- Replace vague verbs (*be*, *have*, *make*, *do*) with a precise verb.
- Replace a noun built from a verb with the verb: *configure* rather than
  *perform the configuration of*.

## Tone in errors and problems

- Describe what happened and what the reader can do, in that order.
- Keep blame off the reader. Passive voice is acceptable here: *The file
  couldn't be saved* rather than *You failed to save the file*.
- Use *sorry* only for a serious problem the reader didn't cause. For a problem
  outside the product's control, state plainly what went wrong.
- Use exclamation points and humor rarely, and never in error or warning text.

## Final read

Before calling a draft done:

- Read it aloud, then read it backward one sentence at a time.
- Read only the headings, then only the first sentence of each paragraph. They
  should tell the whole story without gaps or repeats.
- Try deleting each sentence, paragraph, and section. Leave out anything nobody
  would miss.
- Check that the voice is warm and relaxed, crisp and clear, and ready to help.
